About me

I study how the ocean has changed over the past century and what that reveals about its response to climate change. I apply generative machine learning, mainly score-based diffusion models, to combine climate model simulations with sparse historical observations and reconstruct the past ocean, including its interior, with quantified uncertainty.

A few questions guide my research. How has the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) evolved since the late 19th century, and how much of that evolution is forced rather than internal variability? What can sparse historical observations tell us about the ocean below the surface? Can generative models trained on climate simulations turn them into reconstructions with reliable uncertainty? So far, our reconstructions show no significant AMOC decline over the 20th century, but a weakening over recent decades.

I'm a PhD student in the School of Oceanography at the University of Washington, advised by Georgy Manucharyan and Kyle Armour, and a NASA FINESST Fellow. Before UW, I received a B.S. in physics and applied mathematics and an M.S. in atmospheric physics from Lomonosov Moscow State University.

Research

Reconstructing the historical 3D ocean with generative data assimilation

Below the surface, the ocean was measured only sparsely before the 2000s, so we know little about how the ocean interior changed during the 20th century. We train a diffusion model on climate model simulations so that it learns what realistic ocean states look like and how they evolve. We then guide it with the observations that do exist, namely sea surface temperature, air temperature over land, sea level pressure, and temperature and salinity profiles. The result is a set of plausible global 3D ocean states for every month from 1950 to 2020, and the spread between them shows how uncertain the reconstruction is. The approach builds on score-based data assimilation (SDA) by Rozet and Louppe (2023), and the diffusion model uses NVIDIA's cBottle architecture, adapted to the coupled ocean and atmosphere.

How has the AMOC changed since 1900?

The AMOC is a system of ocean currents that carries heat northward in the Atlantic. Continuous direct measurements began only in 2004, so its earlier history is debated. A widely used estimate based on sea surface temperature in a small region south of Greenland suggests that the AMOC weakened substantially during the 20th century. We trained neural networks on 18 climate models to estimate AMOC strength from sea surface temperature and sea surface height across the whole Atlantic, and applied them to observations. We find no significant change over the 20th century, but a weakening over recent decades that agrees with the direct measurements.
